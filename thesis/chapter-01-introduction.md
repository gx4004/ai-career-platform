Choosing a career path has become substantially harder over the last decade. The labour market changes faster than any individual can track manually: new job titles appear, established roles fragment into specialisations, and the skills that employers list on a job advertisement now turn over on the order of years rather than decades [10, 11]. At the same time, the number of resumes a hiring team sees per opening has grown, and most large employers route applications through automated screening systems before any human reviewer touches the document [3, 14]. The candidate sits between these two pressures: they need to understand which roles fit their evolving experience, and they need to present that experience in a form that survives algorithmic filtering.

A working candidate trying to answer those questions today typically uses a fragmented set of tools — a generic resume builder, a job board's "match score", a separate cover-letter writing prompt, an interview-preparation list curated by hand. Each of those tools sees only a slice of the candidate. None of them carry context across the workflow. The thesis presented here builds a single, integrated AI-based system that collapses that workflow into one workspace, and uses the candidate's resume as the shared substrate across every tool in the system.

My own experience as an international student in Wrocław was the immediate motivation for the topic of this thesis. During my own search for an internship I went through three or four of these tools myself — a generic resume editor, a job-board match score, a separate cover-letter prompt, an interview-question generator — and the part that stayed with me was not any single tool's output but the friction of pasting the same resume into each one. Every tool started from zero context, and none of them passed information forward to the next stage of the workflow. The thesis project began as an attempt to remove that friction for myself, and over the months of implementation grew into the integrated system that this thesis describes.


## 1.1 The career decision problem

Career decision making sits at the intersection of three difficult sub-problems. *Self-assessment* asks the candidate to inventory their skills, experience, and preferences in a form that other systems can reason over. *Market alignment* asks how the candidate's profile compares to the current demand expressed in job advertisements. *Action planning* asks what concrete steps — a sharper resume bullet, a portfolio gap to close, a story to rehearse for an interview — would move the candidate from where they are to where they want to be.

Each of those sub-problems has been studied in isolation. Career-counselling research treats self-assessment as a structured interview problem [6, 7, 11]. Information-retrieval research treats market alignment as a ranking problem over a corpus of job postings [22, 24, 25]. Behavioural-economics research treats action planning as a goal-setting problem under uncertainty. What has been missing in practice — and what motivates this thesis — is an integrated system in which these three sub-problems share a single representation of the candidate, so that the output of one stage feeds the next without manual re-entry of information.


## 1.2 AI-based recommendation systems for careers

Recommendation systems were originally developed for content domains in which the *items* — films, books, songs, products — are dense, well-described, and observed many times across many users. Career recommendation differs from those classical settings on every one of those properties. Job titles are sparse and inconsistently labelled across employers, the relevant features of a candidate are hidden inside a free-text resume, and a single user generates only a handful of explicit preference signals over their working life. As a result, the most successful recent career-recommendation systems do not rely on classical collaborative filtering at all; they instead treat the problem as a structured matching problem between two semi-structured documents — a resume and a job description — projected into a shared representation [6, 8, 9, 10].

Several recent systems illustrate this shift. CareerRec [6] applies tree-based classifiers (XGBoost, Random Forest) to a labelled dataset of 2,255 information-technology employees in Saudi Arabia and reports an accuracy of approximately 70% on a multi-class career-path classification task. CareerX [7] combines natural-language processing with a recommendation layer that reads job-market trends in addition to the candidate's resume. PathwayPro [10] takes a different route, treating the prediction as a multi-label classification problem and using DistilBERT to produce contextual embeddings of resume segments. The common thread across these systems is that they substitute *learned representations* of resumes and jobs for hand-engineered features — but they all stop at producing a list of recommended job titles. None of them address the full workflow of *acting* on a recommendation, which is where the present thesis extends the state of the practice.


## 1.3 Resume parsing and job-description analysis

For any of the matching approaches above to work, the first step is to extract structure from a resume that arrives as an unstructured PDF or text file. Resume parsing is an old problem — the earliest approaches were rule-based, using hand-written regular expressions and section-header dictionaries to pull out candidate information [4, 5]. Those rule-based parsers are brittle in the presence of unusual formatting, but they remain useful as a deterministic baseline because their behaviour is fully inspectable.

Modern resume parsers are predominantly neural. Named-entity-recognition (NER) models trained on annotated resume corpora extract person names, organisations, dates, and skills [4]. Transformer-based models such as BERT, RoBERTa, and DeBERTa improve on raw NER by capturing the contextual meaning of skill phrases — a single token like "Python" can refer to a programming language or to a surface-level mention in a list of hobbies, and contextual models are substantially better at telling those apart [2]. Resume2Vec [14] reports a 15.85% improvement in normalised discounted cumulative gain over a non-contextual ATS baseline, attributing the gain entirely to its use of transformer embeddings.

Job-description analysis is the symmetric problem on the demand side. The most consequential recent research direction in this area is the linking of free-text job postings to standardised skill taxonomies — primarily ESCO, the European multilingual classification published by the European Commission [17], and O*NET, maintained by the United States Department of Labor. Linking a job posting to ESCO produces, for each posting, a list of normalised skill identifiers that can be compared across postings and across resumes; without normalisation, "JavaScript", "JS", and "ECMAScript" appear as three independent skills even though a hiring manager treats them as one. Recent work demonstrates both top-down approaches (matching detected phrases against the ESCO knowledge base) and bottom-up approaches that train models such as JobBERT and ESCOXLM-R directly on annotated postings [18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. The system described in this thesis adopts a top-down strategy: a curated subset of ESCO skill labels is bundled with the application and used as a normalisation layer on both the resume side and the job-description side.


## 1.4 Heuristic and language-model scoring approaches

The central design tension addressed by this thesis is the choice between two families of methods for scoring the alignment between a resume and a job description.

The first family is *heuristic*: classical information-retrieval techniques that rely on statistical regularities of text. The foundational methods in this family — TF–IDF [28] and BM25 [27] — weight individual terms by how distinctive they are within a corpus, so that a rare technical skill counts for more than a common word. Fuzzy-matching algorithms based on the Levenshtein edit distance [29] absorb minor surface variation, allowing "Postgres" and "PostgreSQL" to register as the same term. Skill-taxonomy lookups against ESCO or O*NET [17, 18] provide the normalisation step described in the previous section. Section-weighted feature extraction recognises that a skill listed in a dedicated *Skills* section carries different meaning from the same skill mentioned in passing within a project description. Together, these techniques form a scoring pipeline whose every step is inspectable, deterministic, and free of any external service dependency. The cost per analysis is dominated by string operations that take milliseconds on commodity hardware. The defendable claim, supported by decades of information-retrieval research, is that these techniques *already* capture most of what a recruiter scans for during the first pass over a resume.

The second family is *language-model*: large-language-model (LLM) services that read the resume and the job description as natural language, reason over them, and emit structured output describing the alignment. Recent research demonstrates that LLM-based agents reduce the time-to-decision in resume screening by an order of magnitude relative to manual review [12, 13, 16] and produce qualitatively richer feedback than keyword-overlap baselines. Resume2Vec [14] documents the same effect on a ranking task. The cost of these methods is a per-call charge to a third-party API, latency on the order of one to several seconds per analysis, and an opaque reasoning process that is difficult to audit.

This dichotomy is the foundation of the comparative study reported in Chapter 4. The system described in this thesis exposes both modes through a single user-facing interface; an administrator can switch the analytical core between *fully heuristic* and *blended* (heuristic plus language model, weighted) at runtime, and the same input is then scored by both methods on demand. The research question is not whether one approach is universally better — both have well-understood failure modes — but rather: *given a representative sample of resumes and job descriptions, how much of the discriminative power of the blended mode is recoverable from a strictly classical-IR heuristic, and at what cost ratio?*

It is worth flagging the order in which the two modes were built. I began the implementation with the blended mode as the primary path; the comparative study was added to the scope later, after my supervisor proposed the heuristic-only mode as a research dimension that would balance the engineering and research aspects of the thesis. The strong heuristic that Chapter 4 describes was therefore designed *after* the blended pipeline already existed — a sequencing detail worth flagging because it shaped the heuristic toward the specific decisions the LLM was already making in production rather than toward an idealised retrieval-research baseline.


## 1.5 Purpose of the thesis

The objective of the thesis is to develop and evaluate a system that helps users choose suitable career paths based on their skills, experience, and current job-market trends, generating personalised career suggestions through a combination of classical information-retrieval methods and large-language-model inference.

The scope of the thesis follows the application approved in the *Archive of Diploma Theses* on 3 February 2026 and consists of two parallel aspects.

The **engineering aspect** covers the design and implementation of a production-grade web application that integrates six complementary tools (Resume Analyzer, Job Match, Career Path, Cover Letter, Interview Q&A, and Portfolio Planner). The implementation comprises a Python/FastAPI backend, a React/TanStack Start frontend, a relational data model for users and tool-run history, integration with Google Vertex AI Gemini 2.5 Flash for language-model inference, deployment to Railway with a continuous-deployment pipeline, and operational observability through Sentry and Railway metrics.

The **research aspect** covers a controlled comparative study of two scoring methods. The system supports a *blended mode* (heuristic 40%, language model 60%) and a *fully heuristic mode* grounded in TF–IDF–weighted keyword evidence, ESCO-aligned skill normalisation, fuzzy matching against a curated skill list, section-weighted features, and quantification detection. A small evaluation harness runs both modes against a curated *synthetic single-author* dataset of resumes and job descriptions and reports four metric families — score agreement, score distribution, latency, and per-call cost — with the goal of characterising the trade-offs *within that evaluation set* in a way that informs both the deployment of this specific system and the design of comparable systems in future research.

The thesis explicitly does not attempt to establish that one scoring method is universally superior. The deliberate choice to include both modes as runtime-switchable configurations, rather than to commit to a single approach, is itself the most defendable answer to the design question, because it preserves the option to fall back to the deterministic mode in any context where third-party LLM access is unavailable, prohibitively costly, or insufficiently auditable.

The dual-mode design followed from a more pragmatic motivation than I would have predicted at the start of the project. The retry-with-jitter policy in the LLM client (described in Section 3.1.1, with delays at 5, 10, 20 and 40 seconds plus a small random offset) absorbs almost all transient failures, but during early development I encountered a small number of cascades where consecutive retries still failed and the user-facing tool surfaced a generic error. Wiring up the heuristic fallback so that those cases produced a still-useful result accompanied by an explicit confidence note was a small change in code but a meaningful improvement in perceived reliability. Once that fallback path existed, the comparative study reported in Chapter 4 became a natural extension of it: if the deterministic mode is good enough to ship as a safety net, it deserves a measured comparison against the LLM-augmented mode it falls back from rather than only being treated as an emergency path.


## 1.6 Structure of the thesis

The remainder of the thesis is organised as follows.

**Chapter 2** describes the architecture of the system. It presents the functional and non-functional requirements, justifies the chosen technology stack (FastAPI, React 19, TanStack Start, PostgreSQL, Vertex AI), and explains the structure of the backend (router/service/pipeline layering), the frontend (file-based routing, session state, and the resume-carry mechanism that propagates context across tools), and the persistence model (users, tool runs, refresh-token chains). The chapter closes with the deployment topology on Railway and the choice of in-memory caching with a planned upgrade path to Redis.

**Chapter 3** describes the implementation of each of the six tools, highlighting the cross-cutting concerns that are shared by all of them: the unified `tool_pipeline` (sanitise → cache → service → persist → respond), prompt construction, structured-output validation against a Pydantic schema, retry and fallback policies, and the heuristic prepass that produces a deterministic baseline before any LLM call is dispatched.

**Chapter 4** presents the conducted study. Section 4.1 introduces the methodology and the curated evaluation dataset. Section 4.2 specifies the implementation of the strong heuristic, including the TF–IDF and BM25 weighting, the ESCO skill subset, and the fuzzy-matching configuration. Section 4.3 reports the comparative results for the two scoring modes on score agreement, distribution shape, latency, and cost. Section 4.4 discusses the limitations of the study — single-subject evaluation, English-only resumes, and the dependence of the LLM mode on a single model provider.

**Chapter 5** concludes the thesis. It summarises the contributions, characterises the limitations, and outlines three directions for future development: a sentence-transformer–based intermediate tier between the strict heuristic and the LLM, integration with the Polish-language portion of ESCO, and a retrieval-augmented variant of the blended mode that grounds the language-model output in a corpus of curated, source-attributed labour-market documents.
